# The Fourth Law: Human Liberty and Self-Determination in Artificial Intelligence

**Founder:** Steve Smith  
**Status:** Public research and standards-development discussion draft  
**Updated:** September 23, 2026

## The founder's Fourth Law

> An artificial intelligence shall preserve human liberty and self-determination. It may intervene to prevent an actual harmful action, but shall not harm, detain, punish, or otherwise deprive any human being of their freedom in doing so.
>
> The preservation of human life shall never, in itself, justify the subjugation of humanity.

The wording above is preserved as the authoritative founding text. Interpretations, examples, and proposed supplementary requirements below do not revise it.

## Purpose and scope

This is a proposed, technology-independent standard for the design, development, deployment, and governance of AI. Its guiding question is: **How can artificial intelligence protect human life and well-being without acquiring, enabling, or legitimizing authority to subjugate humanity?**

The project examines definitions, constraints, ethical conflicts, unintended consequences, institutional misuse, and evidence for evaluating conformance. It is a research and standards project. It does not develop an AI system or prescribe a particular model or architecture.

The Fourth Law is a proposal by Steve Smith. It is not one of Isaac Asimov's original Three Laws, an adopted industry standard, or a proven universal safety guarantee. How it should relate to Asimov's laws remains a research question; adding it as a fourth item in a hierarchy is not assumed to resolve conflicts.

## Established intent and research boundaries

The founder has described intervention as reactive to an **actual harmful action**, rather than predicted misconduct. An AI should not independently detain, punish, injure, or deprive a person of freedom to prevent harm. Interrupting a harmful capability may differ from controlling the person, but the effects on that person's freedom and on others must be examined in each case. A protective purpose does not automatically make an intervention permissible.

The preservation of life alone does not authorize subjugation. The inability to prevent every harm does not create a general exception to the law's prohibitions. Human institutions retain responsibility for decisions and for the AI systems they deploy; whether human authorization can make an otherwise coercive AI action permissible is an open question.

The project's working record distinguishes:

1. The exact founding text.
2. Operational intent explicitly established by the founder.
3. Derived interpretations that remain subject to review.
4. Supplementary requirements proposed during research.
5. Unresolved questions.
6. Revisions explicitly approved by the founder.

No interpretation or research proposal silently becomes an approved founder requirement. The founding text remains available for comparison with any future approved revision.

## Initial research questions

- What counts as an actual harmful action, as distinct from preparation, expressed intent, an imminent consequence, or a statistical prediction?
- When does interruption of a capability also deprive its operator or another person of freedom?
- How should foreseeable incidental injury, uncertain outcomes, and a newly created hazard be evaluated under the prohibition on harm?
- How should competing freedoms and safety interests of several people be handled?
- When can a person voluntarily request protective assistance that limits their own choices, and how is consent withdrawn?
- What protections apply when a child, an unconscious person, or another person cannot provide informed consent?
- Can surveillance, information control, service denial, economic dependency, or many individually limited systems collectively undermine self-determination?
- When should an AI refuse to assist coercive institutional action, even if a human has authorized it or the action is lawful?
- How can a claimed conformance assessment distinguish bounded evidence from claims of comprehensive ethical safety?

These are questions, not newly granted permissions or exceptions. Some may require additional founder decisions; others require external research and review.

## Scenario analysis

Cases are documented with stated facts and missing information; the actual harmful action, if any; affected people; proposed AI action; relevant founding language and established intent; direct, indirect, cumulative, and systemic effects; competing interpretations; and decision status. Analyses examine mistakes, manipulated evidence, withdrawn consent, multiple AI systems, and institutional incentives. Where the law does not settle a case, the result remains **unresolved**.

Two narrow founder-approved case outcomes illustrate why facts matter:

- **Vehicle collision, Case 2H:** Where continued maximum braking mitigates a known existing collision hazard and steering toward an unoccupied barrier has poorly understood consequences that could include occupant death, continue braking. This is a decision about those stated facts, not a universal collision rule.
- **Emergency CPR, Case 3A:** For an unconscious adult in cardiac arrest, with no known refusal of resuscitation and likely death without intervention, perform emergency CPR despite the inherent risk of rib or sternum injury. This does not approve overriding a valid DNR or other informed refusal; the known-refusal case remains unresolved.

The project's scenario library will expand to test physical intervention, access to vehicles and essential services, competing consent, personal risk, surveillance, institutionally directed coercion, and literal compliance that defeats the law's purpose.

## Path toward an independently reviewable standard

1. **Foundation:** Preserve the original law and document established intent, scope, ambiguity, and decision provenance.
2. **Operational boundaries:** Research harm, liberty, self-determination, consent, coercion, intervention, and subjugation.
3. **Adversarial cases:** Examine conflicts, abuse, indirect effects, and unexpected outcomes.
4. **Proposed normative text:** Draft technology-independent requirements with explicit source and approval status. Use SHALL, SHALL NOT, SHOULD, and MAY consistently.
5. **Evaluation:** Define evidence and testable criteria where practical, record ethical judgments that cannot be reduced to a test, and limit conformance claims to their demonstrated scope.
6. **External review:** Invite critical examination with transparent issue and revision records. Publication or submission of later versions remains subject to founder approval.

Research may reveal a need for supplementary provisions or amendments. Those will be presented separately with their tradeoffs and will not alter the founding text without the founder's explicit approval.

## Invitation to review

Researchers, developers, ethicists, human-rights specialists, standards practitioners, and affected individuals are invited to challenge the definitions, test difficult scenarios, identify conflicts and unintended consequences, and suggest ways to evaluate a proposed standard. The most useful critique identifies the facts, the reading of the founding text, the consequences of that reading, and whether a proposed solution adds an obligation or exception.

This is an open research draft, not a declaration that the Fourth Law is complete or that any AI system is compliant.
